# Research & Claims Ledger: Brain Plasticity & Repetition Without Repetition

| Claim ID | Claim Statement | Neuroscience & Biomechanical Support | Limits & Nuances | Script Placement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **C01** | Doing identical drill repetitions gives a false sense of learning (fluency illusion), but limits robust skill transfer. | Bjork & Bjork (Desirable Difficulties); Shea & Morgan (1979) on contextual interference; blocked vs. random practice. | Blocked practice produces rapid immediate performance gains, but poor long-term retention and adaptability. | Scene 1 (0–311f) |
| **C02** | Soviet neurophysiologist Nikolai Bernstein tracked master blacksmiths and found joint trajectories vary on every single strike despite identical impact locations. | Bernstein, N. A. (1967). *The Co-ordination and Regulation of Movements*. Pergamon Press; Kymocyclography studies of blacksmith hammering (1930s). | Measured tip trajectory variability is smaller than joint angle variability, demonstrating motor synergy and redundancy. | Scene 2 (311–814f) |
| **C03** | Bernstein coined "repetition without repetition" to describe solving the motor degrees of freedom problem under changing conditions. | Turvey (1990); Latash (2012) *The Bliss of Motor Abundance*; Dynamic Systems Theory in motor control. | The repetition is of the process of solving the motor problem, not of the mechanical movement itself. | Scene 2 (709–814f) |
| **C04** | The brain does not store rigid, robotic motor tracks; neuroplasticity is driven by active error-correction and problem solving. | Merzenich & Jenkins (Experience-Dependent Plasticity); Kleim & Jones (2008) principles of neural plasticity; Hebbian learning with neuromodulatory gating. | Autopilot/habitual execution engages basal ganglia with minimal cortical remodeling once errors cease. | Scene 3 (814–1189f) |
| **C05** | True mastery requires training the nervous system to handle changing internal (fatigue, muscle tension) and external conditions. | Schmidt & Lee (2019) *Motor Control and Learning*; Schöllhorn et al. (Differential Learning). | Novel variations must still be functionally task-relevant to avoid degrading the core attractor state. | Scene 4 (1189–1332f) |
| **C06** | Practice variability (varying stance, tempo, angle) forces continuous neural engagement and creates resilient, transferable mastery. | Wulf & Shea (2002); Davids et al. (Constraints-Led Approach to motor learning). | Novices need an initial gross movement coordination pattern before high variability is introduced. | Scene 5 (1332–1760f) |
